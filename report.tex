\begin{abstract}
We present a multi-camera pipeline for tracking players, referees and the
ball during a basketball action recorded at the Sanbapolis facility, and for
reconstructing their 3D trajectories via triangulation. Bounding boxes are
annotated at 5\,fps on three synchronized camera views and used both to train
the detectors and as ground truth. Players and referees are tracked
independently per camera at the native 25\,fps with a generic YOLO detector
and its built-in tracker, reaching Precision~$=0.976$, Recall~$=0.819$
(F1~$=0.890$) and a mean matched IoU of $0.592$ on the annotated subset. The
ball, which the generic detector fails to recover at this scale, is instead
tracked with a YOLOv8m model fine-tuned on a ball-only dataset and evaluated
with SAHI tiled inference (mAP@50~$=0.779$, mAP@50-95~$=0.430$ at training
time). Cameras are calibrated from manually surveyed court correspondences
via a normalized DLT solve refined with a distortion-aware nonlinear
least-squares step (RMS reprojection error between $1.7$ and $5.5$\,px).
2D tracks are re-identified across views with a vote-based consensus scheme
and triangulated into a court-centered world frame; against pseudo-ground
truth obtained by triangulating the annotated boxes, the reconstruction
achieves a median Euclidean error of $0.43$\,m for players and referees, and
$2.26$\,m for the ball, the latter limited by the small number of frames in
which the ball is jointly visible in at least two views.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This work addresses the Player Tracking project of the Computer Vision
course: given a multi-camera setup at the Sanbapolis sports facility, the
goal is to detect and track players, referees and the ball in each camera
view, and to reconstruct their 3D trajectories by triangulation across
views. One action was recorded from three fixed, synchronized cameras
(\texttt{out2}, \texttt{out4}, \texttt{out13}) on a FIBA-regulation
$28\times15$\,m basketball court. As a team of two, the mandatory scope
covers annotation (task~1), 2D tracking with numerical evaluation
(task~2/2a), and 3D triangulation with numerical evaluation (task~3/3a)
using at least two camera views; the Unreal Engine visualization (task~4)
is left as bonus future work (Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

Two aspects of the setup make the problem harder than single-camera
tracking. First, the ball is a small, fast-moving object that is frequently
occluded or motion-blurred, and is easily missed by a detector tuned for
person-scale objects. Second, associating a track across cameras requires a
reliable geometric model of the scene: reusing a per-camera object ID
across views is not possible, so cross-view correspondence has to be
recovered from projective geometry rather than from appearance alone (no
re-identification features were introduced in the interest of time).

This report describes: (i) the annotation protocol and resulting dataset,
(ii) a camera calibration procedure based on manually surveyed
correspondences, (iii) a two-track 2D detection/tracking pipeline --- a
generic detector for players/referees and a dedicated small-object pipeline
for the ball --- and (iv) a cross-camera association and multi-view
triangulation module, evaluated quantitatively against the annotated ground
truth.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Dataset and Annotation}
\label{sec:dataset}

One basketball action was annotated from the three camera views on
Roboflow, at 5\,fps, yielding $309$ frames and $2638$ bounding boxes over
$16$ categories: a generic \texttt{person} catch-all class, \texttt{Ball},
six jersey-numbered identities for the ``Red'' team (\texttt{Red\_2},
\texttt{Red\_7}, \texttt{Red\_9}, \texttt{Red\_11}, \texttt{Red\_13},
\texttt{Red\_23}), six for the ``White'' team, and two referees
(\texttt{Refree\_1}, \texttt{Refree\_2}). Annotating individual jersey
numbers, rather than a single \texttt{player} class, lets the 2D evaluation
(Sec.~\ref{sec:2d_results}) and the pseudo-ground-truth triangulation
(Sec.~\ref{sec:3d_results}) match a specific person across frames and
cameras without needing a separate re-identification step. As required for
a two-person team, at least two views (\texttt{out2}, \texttt{out4}) are
used for the mandatory deliverable; the third view (\texttt{out13}) was
additionally calibrated and used to strengthen cross-camera triangulation.

\subsection{Camera Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

Each camera is calibrated independently from 12 manually surveyed
world-to-image correspondences (court lines, free-throw markings and
backboard/rim points), expressed in a court-centered world frame with the
origin at the court center, the $z$-axis pointing upward, the $y$-axis
towards the benches, and the $x$-axis completing a right-handed frame.

Correspondences are Hartley-normalized~\cite{Hartley2004} (points
centered at the centroid, mean distance $\sqrt{2}$ resp.\ $\sqrt{3}$ from
the origin) before building the direct linear transform (DLT) system
$A\mathbf{p}=0$, whose solution is the right singular vector of $A$
associated with the smallest singular value. The resulting projection
matrix $P_{\text{norm}}$ is de-normalized and factored as $P = K[R\,|\,t]$
via RQ decomposition, fixing the sign ambiguity so that $K$ has a positive
diagonal and $\det(R)=+1$.

This linear estimate is then refined by nonlinear least squares
(Levenberg--Marquardt with a Huber loss, via \texttt{scipy.optimize}),
jointly optimizing the intrinsics, the extrinsics (rotation as a rotation
vector), and a radial/tangential distortion model $(k_1,k_2,p_1,p_2)$
against the reprojection error of the same correspondences. Table
\ref{tab:calib} reports the final RMS reprojection error per camera.

\subsection{2D Detection and Tracking}
\label{sec:2d_tracking}

\paragraph{Players and referees.} Each camera is processed independently
at the native $25$\,fps with a generic YOLO detector (COCO classes
\texttt{person} and \texttt{sports ball}), run on frames downscaled to
$1920\times1088$, using the detector's built-in tracker
(\texttt{persist=True}, a ByteTrack-style~\cite{Zhang2022} association) to
maintain identities over time. A low confidence threshold ($0.1$) is used
at detection time to favor recall, relying on the tracker to filter
spurious detections through temporal persistence rather than discarding
them upfront.

\paragraph{Ball.} The same generic pipeline is largely blind to the ball
at this scale (Sec.~\ref{sec:2d_results}): at $1920\times1088$ the ball
covers only a handful of pixels and is frequently confused with background
clutter or missed entirely. A first attempt used a classical
color-and-motion baseline (HSV thresholding combined with frame
differencing and an area filter) but proved too sensitive to lighting
changes and motion blur to be usable. The final pipeline instead
fine-tunes a YOLOv8m model on a ball-only subset of the annotated dataset
at a high input resolution (\texttt{imgsz}$=3840$), and applies
SAHI~\cite{Akyon2022} tiled inference at test time ($1500\times1500$\,px
slices, $10\%$ overlap) so that the ball is detected at full resolution
even though the source frames are far larger than the network input.

\subsection{Cross-Camera Association and 3D Triangulation}
\label{sec:3d_tracking}

Per-camera 2D tracks are first undistorted using each camera's calibrated
distortion coefficients. For every camera pair, and independently for
every frame, candidate cross-camera matches between per-camera track IDs
are scored by triangulating each pair of detections and measuring the
combined reprojection error in both views; a Hungarian assignment then
selects the best pairing per frame, keeping only pairs with reprojection
error below $30$\,px. Because a single frame's assignment can be wrong
(occlusions, symmetric jerseys), a track-level global identity is only
formed from pairs that are \emph{mutually} the best match and that win the
per-frame assignment in at least $5$ distinct frames across the whole
sequence; consistent pairs are merged into cross-camera groups with a
union-find structure, yielding one global ID per group.

For every frame, detections sharing a global ID from at least two cameras
are triangulated with a multi-view DLT solve. A court-sized plausibility
box (the $28\times15$\,m court extended by a $5$\,m margin for players near
the sideline, with height in $[-1,3]$\,m) discards triangulated points that
fall outside it, which in practice removes the residual wrong cross-camera
matches rather than genuine player positions.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All results are computed against the annotations described in
Sec.~\ref{sec:dataset} (5\,fps ground truth, $309$ frames, $2638$ boxes).
The 2D evaluation matches predicted and ground-truth boxes per frame with a
Hungarian assignment on IoU and reports standard detection/tracking
metrics (Precision, Recall, F1, and MOTP as the mean IoU over matched
pairs). The 3D evaluation triangulates the annotated 2D boxes themselves
(whenever a person or the ball is annotated in $\geq2$ views in the same
frame) to obtain a pseudo-ground-truth 3D position, and matches it to the
reconstructed trajectory with a Hungarian assignment on 3D Euclidean
distance, accepting matches within $3$\,m.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We built and evaluated a complete two-camera pipeline for player/referee
and ball tracking with 3D reconstruction on a basketball court, covering
the mandatory tasks for a two-person team: annotation, per-camera 2D
tracking with numerical evaluation, and multi-view 3D triangulation with
numerical evaluation. Players and referees are tracked reliably in 2D
(F1~$=0.890$) and localized in 3D with sub-half-meter median error
($0.43$\,m), validating both the calibration procedure and the
vote-based cross-camera association scheme. The ball proved to be the
hard case throughout the pipeline: the generic detector misses it almost
entirely, the dedicated SAHI+YOLOv8m pipeline recovers it far better at
training time but still yields few simultaneous multi-view detections, and
its resulting 3D error is dominated by this scarcity of matches rather
than by triangulation accuracy.

Future work includes: extending the 2D and 3D evaluation to the second
mandatory camera (\texttt{out4}) and consolidating results across all
three calibrated views; a dedicated 2D bounding-box evaluation of the ball
pipeline (Table~\ref{tab:ball_train} currently reports only training-time
accuracy); improving ball recall across views (e.g., lower confidence with
stronger cross-view geometric gating, or a temporal ball-specific
tracker); trajectory-level 3D metrics for task~3a beyond static point
matching (e.g., smoothness, ID switches); and, as a bonus, importing the
reconstructed motion into Unreal Engine (task~4).
